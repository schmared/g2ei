Septuagint base · exhaustive interpretation · not a translation

Ἐν ἀρχῇ
as far as English can be made to carry it

Every load-bearing word is opened to the full range its lexicon gives it, and left open — no single English equivalent is chosen, because choosing one is where the meaning goes. Three parallel readings run against each verse: the apparatus, the unanchored text, and the anchored text. The Old Testament is read from the Greek throughout, so that when the New Testament quotes it, the quotation is visible on the page.

Conventions

Unanchored

The headword is removed entirely. The reader rebuilds the referent from the gloss-chain alone. Best for poetry, prologue, and theological argument, where the familiar word is exactly what stops the reader from seeing.

Anchored

Each word is headed by the first sense its lexicon gives, in the form the text uses, and its chain trails behind, set off by dashes and shaded. Read only the unshaded words for a plain word-for-word rendering; the chains are word for word the unanchored text. Best for narrative, law, and genealogy, where a reader needs to hold a referent across many verses without losing the thread.

Names

Transliterated names keep the form the Greek gives them, with the meaning in parentheses immediately after, in both reading texts. A name left unglossed is a hole in the sentence — it is a word that meant something to the first hearer and means nothing now.

Verbal aspect

Where the Greek aspect carries weight English cannot, it is stated in the text rather than footnoted: one completed act seen whole for the aorist, and kept on being for the imperfect.

The apparatus

Hebrew-to-Greek divergences stay in the notes and never enter the reading text. The Septuagint's own reading is the text; where it parts from the Masoretic Hebrew, that is a fact about the text, not a defect to be quietly repaired.

What is not added

No sense is imported that the Greek word does not carry, however familiar it is from other English versions. Where a well-known English phrase has no basis in this text, the notes say so. Nor are capitals added: the manuscripts have none, so no word is capitalised to make it a title.

Word by word

Under each Greek line, every word with its dictionary form, what its own form adds — tense, voice, mood, case — and the opening senses of its lexicon entry, in the lexicon's order. A concordance stops at the dictionary form; this takes the step after it.

Notes

Lexical range, aspect, and where the Greek parts from the Hebrew

ἐν ἀρχῇ
ἀρχή holds together two things English must split: the temporal origin-point, and ruling precedence. The second sense is alive in ἄρχων, ruler, and in the ἀρχ- of every English word from monarch to architect. Neither sense is optional here.
ἐποίησεν
Aorist active indicative, 3rd singular, of ποιέω — the everyday verb for making and doing, used of potters, poets, and God alike. divergence Hebrew bārā' is a verb that in this stem never takes a human subject. ποιέω takes any subject at all. The exclusivity — this is a thing only God does — is not in the Greek.
ὁ θεός
Articular: the God, the known and definite one. Renders Hebrew Elohim, plural in form but taking singular agreement. Greek has no way to show that and does not try.
τὸν οὐρανόν
divergence Singular. Hebrew šāmayim is a dual or plural form — heavens. The LXX collapses it to one vaulted arch.
τὴν γῆν
γῆ runs from soil underfoot, to farmland, to a country, to dry land as against the sea, to the whole terrestrial realm. Pairing it with οὐρανός pulls toward the widest sense without cancelling the narrow ones.
Capitals
God is capitalised because the lexicon prints it so. The pronouns standing for God in the chain are not: he who is God definitively, the one the article marks out. The manuscripts have no lower case, so a reverential capital is never in the text — it is an editorial decision, and this rendering does not make it.

Notes

Lexical range, aspect, and where the Greek parts from the Hebrew

δέ
A soft adversative, not the hard ἀλλά. It pivots the narrative without contradicting what came before: however, whereas, on the other hand.
ἦν
Imperfect of εἰμί. Durative — a condition that held and kept holding, not a snapshot. English was is aspectually blank, so the duration has to be spelled out.
ἀόρατος καὶ ἀκατασκεύαστος
divergence The single largest departure in the verse, and possibly in the chapter. Hebrew tōhû wā-bōhû is desolation and emptiness — trackless waste, ruin, the wilderness where nothing is. The Greek says invisible and un-outfitted: a thing that exists but is neither perceptible nor yet furnished. On this reading Philo and much of the patristic tradition built a pre-cosmic, formless substrate. It is not what the Hebrew says.
ἀόρατος
Un-seeable, not presenting itself to sight. The same word Paul uses of God himself — the image of the invisible God, Col 1:15.
ἀκατασκεύαστος
Built on κατασκευή: the full outfitting of a house or a ship — fittings, provisions, installed equipment. Formless is too abstract; the word means not yet fitted out.
ἄβυσσος
a-βυσσος, without a sounding — a depth no plumb line reaches. Renders Hebrew tĕhôm, the primeval deep.
πνεῦμα θεοῦ
Anarthrous. πνεῦμα and Hebrew rûaḥ share the same triple range — wind, breath, spirit — one of the rare places where the two languages line up exactly and nothing at all is lost.
ἐπεφέρετο
divergence Imperfect middle-passive of ἐπιφέρω, to be borne upon. Hebrew mĕraḥepet is a bird word — hovering, fluttering, brooding over young, used of an eagle over the nest in Deut 32:11. The Greek is a current, not a wing.
ἐπάνω τοῦ ὕδατος
not in this text The familiar face of the waters is not here. The Hebrew has face of at both points; the LXX drops it at both. ὕδατος is also singular against Hebrew plural mayim.
Capitals
πνεῦμα is printed lowercase in both reading texts. The manuscripts have no lower case at all, so a capital is never in the text: Spirit with a capital would decide for the reader that the word is a title.
Names
None in this verse.

Notes

Lexical range, aspect, and the grammar English cannot reproduce

Ἐν ἀρχῇ
Word for word the opening of LXX Genesis. John is quoting the Greek Old Testament, not the Hebrew. On a Septuagint base the citation is simply visible on the page instead of buried in a footnote — which is the whole argument for reading the Old Testament in Greek alongside the New.
ἦν
Imperfect of εἰμί — and emphatically not ἐγένετο, came to be, which the prologue reserves for created things from verse 3 onward. The entire architecture of the passage rests on this one contrast, and English was carries none of it. Hence the expansion: was already in unbroken existence without any point of having come to be.
ὁ λόγος
Articular. The range: the spoken word, the utterance, the thing said; the account or reckoning, as in rendering an account; ratio and proportion; reasoned discourse; and in Stoic usage the rational principle ordering the cosmos. English Word keeps the speech and drops the reason. Latin had the same problem — verbum won, though sermo held more.
πρὸς τὸν θεόν
πρός with the accusative is directional: toward, facing, up against. It is not παρά (beside), not μετά (in company with), not σύν (together with) — any of which the writer could have used. English with is static; πρός is vectored. This is the largest single loss in the standard versions.
θεὸς ἦν ὁ λόγος
The predicate θεός stands without the article and is thrown to the front; ὁ λόγος carries the article and is the subject. A fronted anarthrous predicate nominative is normally qualitative — it says what the subject is by nature, not which individual it is identical with. So: not the Word was the Father, which the article placement refuses, and not a god, which the fronting refuses. Word order does work here that no English word order can do, which is why this clause has been fought over for eighteen centuries.
Capitals
λόγος and its chain are printed lowercase. The manuscripts have no lower case, so the capital in the familiar Word is not in the text; it is an editorial decision that the noun is a title.
Names
None. λόγος is expanded, not transliterated.

Notes

Lexical range, aspect, and the naming rule

Ἐγένετο
Aorist middle of γίνομαι — came to be, came into existence, arose. Placed first for emphasis. This is precisely the verb withheld from the Logos in verses 1–2, and it arrives the moment a creature enters the sentence.
ἀπεσταλμένος
Perfect passive participle of ἀποστέλλω — sent off on another's authority, dispatched with a commission. The perfect makes it a standing status, not a past errand. Root of ἀπόστολος.
Ἰωάννης
name A Greek transliteration of Hebrew Yôḥānān, a compound of the divine name with the verb ḥānan, to show favour or be gracious: Yah has shown favour. The name is kept in the Greek form and glossed in parentheses. It is not translated away, because it is what the man was called; it is not left bare, because to the first hearers it was a sentence.
